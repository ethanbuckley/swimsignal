The data feed

For apps, charities and groups that already reach swimmers. SwimSignal's forecasts, the storm overflows behind them, their spill history and the forecast's scored record are files you can read. This page says what is in them, how often they change, what you may reuse and the credit to show.

In short

  • Every listed river and lake spot in England: its pollution risk level for five days, the monitored storm overflows upstream with their live status, their spill history and how the forecast has done there.
  • Any point on a river or lake in England, worked out from the click-anywhere files by an open-source script.
  • Rebuilt hours apart, not every few minutes: scheduled builds have been a median 4.6 to 5.8 hours apart.
  • Most sources allow commercial reuse with a credit. The rain comes from Open-Meteo's free plan, which is for non-commercial use only: a paid feed needs SwimSignal on an Open-Meteo subscription first.
  • A forecast, not a water test. Not a toxin measurement. Not a swimming clearance.
  • No prices here. To talk about a licence, email hello@swimsignal.co.uk or start from For organisations.

What you get

The feed is the set of files the site itself is built from. They are public today, in the site's data/ folder, served by GitHub Pages. Another website can read them: they are sent with Access-Control-Allow-Origin: *. The data files page lists every field. Below, each group of files with one record from it, taken from this build. In the examples, lists are cut to their first entry and some fields are left out.

Each spot's forecast

spots.json holds every listed spot. For each: where it is, and its five days, each with its sewage spill risk (a 0–100 score for sewage from upstream reaching the spot, stored as 0 to 1; it is a score, not a probability) and its pollution risk level. Where a spot has one, a day also has the E. coli estimate: the chance a sample would show over 900 E. coli per 100 ml. It has been tested from May to September only, which in_validated_season says. now says how many overflows upstream are discharging at the last poll, how many report live, and how many cannot be seen. spots-lite.json is the same without each spot's overflows, which are in spot/<id>.json.

The overflows upstream, with live status

upstream/<id>.json lists every monitored storm overflow within 60 km upstream of a spot along the rivers: the company's name and id for it, how far upstream and how long its water takes to arrive, its model weight (a modelled relative contribution, 0 to 1; not a measured concentration and not a chance of contamination), its long-term average spills a year, and what its company's live feed said at the build's poll. overflows.geojson has every overflow on the map, with the same status.

A status is 1 discharging, 0 not discharging, −1 monitor offline, −2 no live feed (all of Dŵr Cymru Welsh Water's overflows: SwimSignal reuses none of its own data, only the Environment Agency's annual returns) or −3 unknown. data_state says whether it is current: a status from a feed that has not updated within 6 hours is stale.

Any point on a river or lake

On the map, a tap anywhere on a river or lake in England gets five days too. There is no file for each point, and no hosted service to ask. The page works out the point's forecast in the browser, from the files in anypoint/: the river network, the overflows that can reach each stretch, and each overflow's chance of spilling on each day. The script that does it, anypoint.js, is open source under the MIT licence, so an app could run it too; it has not been packaged for that. A point has no E. coli estimate, no river gauge and no bathing-water sample. Wales, Scotland and the island of Ireland are not covered.

The files are columns: one position for each overflow, in the order of overflow_ids.json. The example puts together one overflow's values from two of them.

Spill history

Each spot's overflow history page has a CSV beside it, spot/<id>/profile/monitored-storm-overflows.csv: every monitored overflow upstream, with its spills, spill hours and monitor uptime in each year from 2021 to 2025, from the Environment Agency's annual returns. In spots.json, seasonal counts, month by month, the days with a spill recorded at the spot's overflows. research/spill_durations/<id>.json gives how long their spills lasted in 2024 and 2025. These are records of the past, not forecasts.

The scored record

Every forecast the site issues is logged and later scored against what the overflows did. verification_live.csv has one row for each scored overflow, day and lead, so anyone can check the scores. verification_spots_live.csv does the same for each spot's level. spot_scores.json counts the hits, misses and false alarms at each spot's overflows. verification.json holds everything on the Accuracy page.

How often it changes

  • The whole forecast is rebuilt by a scheduled job. It asks to run every 30 minutes, but GitHub runs scheduled jobs when it has room. From 7 October to 6 November 2026 the scheduled builds were a median 4.6 hours apart; 95 in 100 gaps were under 7.7 hours, and the longest was 10.1 hours. From 31 October to 9 November 2026: a median 5.8 hours, at most 10.6 hours. A change to the site's code builds it again in between.
  • A build took a median 14 minutes from 1 to 6 November 2026. generated_at in each file says when its forecast was issued.
  • The live status is read once, at the build. So "not discharging" can be hours old, and a spill that started after the poll is not in the files. Each overflow's feed_updated_at says when its company's feed last updated.
  • The annual returns behind the spill history come once a year. The Environment Agency publishes bathing-water samples days after they are taken.
  • Each build overwrites the files: the public folder keeps only the latest forecast. Browsers may keep a copy for 10 minutes.

What it is not

  • Not a water test. Nothing in the feed was measured in the water at the spot, apart from the Environment Agency's and partners' lab samples, which are dated and labelled.
  • Not a toxin measurement. The algae check is the Environment Agency's sampler's observation on a date; it does not tell harmful algae from harmless kinds.
  • Not a swimming clearance. A low level means only that nothing the forecast looks at flags the day. It covers monitored storm overflows only: not treated sewage from sewage works, farm runoff, wildlife, other bathers or a pollution incident.
  • Not complete. Some overflows have no live monitor, a company's feed can be late or down, and rain forecasts are wrong more often the further ahead they look.

How good it is today: It misses most spills. Its live scores cover 33 days between 29 September and 8 November 2026. At the high risk line, it warned of about one in three spills. About one in two of its warnings were followed by a spill. 990 of the 2,990 warnings that came true were at United Utilities, the one company the spill model learned from. At the other companies, so far, it warned of about three in ten spills. The Accuracy page has the full scores, updated with every build.
